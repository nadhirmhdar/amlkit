Since Federal Decree-Law No. 10 of 2025 came into effect on 14 October 2025 — replacing Decree-Law No. 20 of 2018, with its executive regulations following on 14 December 2025 — UAE DNFBPs have had to take sanctions screening seriously in a way that goes well beyond a one-time check at onboarding. One obligation trips up more small firms than any other: the 24-hour rule.

What the law actually requires

UAE AML law requires financial institutions and DNFBPs to screen against two specific lists: the UAE Local Terrorist List (maintained by the Cabinet through the Executive Office for Control and Non-Proliferation) and the UN Consolidated List. When either list is updated, a DNFBP must apply freezing measures — without delay, and in any case within 24 hours — to any funds or transactions linked to a newly designated person or entity.

That 24-hour window isn't just for new designations. The same screening has to run before onboarding a new customer, at every periodic KYC review, whenever a customer's details change, and before processing a transaction. A DNFBP that only screens once, at account opening, is not meeting the standard.

Missing the window is not a paperwork issue. Failing to freeze a listed party's funds in time, or otherwise providing funds or services to a designated person, is a breach of UAE law that can carry financial penalties and, in serious cases, criminal liability.

Beyond the legal minimum

The Local Terrorist List and UN Consolidated List are the two lists the 24-hour rule attaches to by law. Most DNFBPs screen more broadly than that as a matter of risk-based practice — OFAC's SDN list, EU and UK sanctions, and FATF's high-risk-jurisdiction list — especially firms with cross-border clients, correspondent banking relationships, or customers who themselves deal internationally. It's good practice, not a separate statutory deadline, so it's worth knowing which screening is the legal floor and which is added risk management.

The part generic screening tools miss

A large share of UAE customer and counterparty names are Arabic in origin, and the same name can appear half a dozen different ways in Latin script — Mohammed, Muhammad, Mohamed; honorifics included or dropped; different transliteration conventions entirely. A screening tool built for a Latin-only market will either miss genuine matches (false negatives — the dangerous direction) or bury an operator in false positives severe enough that real matches get rubber-stamped through. Either failure mode defeats the point of screening.

What this looks like day to day

For a DNFBP without an in-house compliance team, meeting this standard manually means registering with the Executive Office for update notifications, re-running every active customer against each list on every update, and keeping an auditable record of when each check ran and what it found — on top of the CDD file, risk scoring and reporting work the rest of AML compliance already demands. groAML was built around exactly that workflow: continuous re-screening against the Local Terrorist List, UN Consolidated List, OFAC, EU, UK and FATF high-risk jurisdictions, with Arabic-aware name matching and an evidence trail for every alert.

Frequently asked questions
What is the UAE Local Terrorist List?
A list of designated individuals and entities maintained by the UAE Cabinet via the Executive Office for Control and Non-Proliferation, alongside the UN Consolidated List, as the two sanctions lists UAE AML law specifically requires screening against.
How often must a DNFBP re-screen its customers?
On every list update, before onboarding, at every periodic KYC review, when customer details change, and before processing a transaction.
What happens if a DNFBP misses the 24-hour deadline?
It's a breach of UAE law that can carry financial penalties and, in serious cases, criminal liability under Federal Decree-Law No. 10 of 2025.
Do UAE DNFBPs need to screen OFAC, EU and UK lists too?
The 24-hour freezing obligation attaches to the Local Terrorist List and UN Consolidated List specifically. OFAC, EU, UK and FATF high-risk-jurisdiction screening is broader risk-based practice, not the same statutory deadline.
