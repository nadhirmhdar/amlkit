Two risk assessments, not one

"Risk assessment" gets used for two different documents, and mixing them up is a common source of confusion in an inspection:

  • a business-wide (institutional) risk assessment — how exposed is your firm, as a whole, to money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing; and
  • a customer risk assessment — how risky is this specific relationship, which determines its CDD tier (see our CDD & KYC guide).

They're connected — the business-wide assessment should shape how you weight factors at the customer level — but they answer different questions and sit in different places in your compliance program.

The business-wide risk assessment

The Executive Regulations (Cabinet Resolution No. 134 of 2025) are understood to require every reporting entity to identify, understand and manage its ML/FT/PF risk, documenting that assessment and keeping it updated, and to cover at least four categories of risk factor. Confirm the exact requirement against the Resolution text. The four categories:

Factor categoryWhat it covers
CustomerThe types of customers you serve — individuals vs. entities, PEP exposure, cash-intensive vs. documented income
Product / serviceThe inherent risk of what you sell — a bullion sale carries different risk than a bookkeeping engagement
Delivery channelFace-to-face vs. remote, direct vs. introduced by a third party
GeographicWhere your customers, their funds and their counterparties are based or connected to, including FATF high-risk and increased-monitoring jurisdictions

The Executive Regulations are understood to require the assessment to take the UAE's own National Risk Assessment into account, not just your internal view of your business, and a supervisor can be expected to look for that. Confirm the exact requirement against the Resolution text rather than relying on this summary.

The four customer risk factors

At the individual customer level, the same four categories get scored for one specific relationship: who the customer is, what product or service they're using, how the relationship was established, and where the geographic risk sits (their nationality, incorporation, and where their funds originate). This is the mechanism that turns "we think this sector is higher risk" at the business-wide level into "this specific customer needs enhanced due diligence" at the file level.

From score to CDD tier

A customer risk assessment isn't useful as a number on its own — it has to translate into an action. The usual pattern is a banded score (low / medium / high) that maps directly to the CDD tier (simplified / standard / enhanced), with specific conditions — a sanctions hit, PEP status, a blacklisted jurisdiction, undisclosed beneficial ownership — forcing a high rating and enhanced due diligence regardless of where the rest of the score lands.

A rating without its reasoning isn't evidence Whatever method produces the score, record why: which factors contributed, how much, and against which version of your methodology. A supervisor asking why a customer was rated medium in one review and high in the next needs a dated, reviewable answer — not "it felt higher risk this time."

Why it's never a one-time document

A risk assessment filed once at onboarding and never revisited stops reflecting reality almost immediately. It needs to move when:

  • the customer's business, ownership or activity changes;
  • new adverse media or a sanctions hit surfaces;
  • the jurisdiction lists themselves change (see our regulatory updates guide for how often that happens); or
  • the review interval tied to the customer's current rating comes due — shorter for high-risk customers than for low-risk ones.

Negative-news checks typically need to run on a tighter cycle than a full periodic review: adverse media is an event that can land the week after onboarding, not something that waits politely for your next scheduled review.

Why risk-scoring methodology is changing industry-wide

Traditional transaction monitoring and customer risk scoring across the banking industry has mostly relied on fixed, rules-based thresholds — a pattern familiar from compliance programs everywhere, DNFBPs included. That is changing at the largest banks. Google Cloud's AML AI, developed in partnership with HSBC, replaces that rules-based transaction monitoring with a machine-learning model that assigns each customer a dynamic risk score and flags which factors drove it. HSBC and Google Cloud have publicly reported substantially more suspicious activity detected and fewer false-positive alerts; we have not independently verified the published figures, so we do not repeat them here.

That's a meaningful data point about where large-institution AML technology is heading, and worth knowing about even if it doesn't change what a DNFBP is required to do today. It does not mean machine-learning scoring is mandatory, or that every firm needs it — a documented, risk-based, explainable methodology is the legal bar, whatever technology produces it.

How groAML's own risk engine works

Honestly, not an ML model groAML's risk engine is a weighted-factor rules engine, not a machine-learning model. Each factor — sanctions/terrorist-list status, PEP status, jurisdiction, business sector, beneficial-ownership transparency, delivery channel, cash intensity, adverse media and legal structure — contributes a documented point value from a documented, versioned and dated ruleset, with a confirmed sanctions hit or a FATF-blacklisted jurisdiction forcing a high rating outright. The total maps to a low/medium/high band, which in turn sets the CDD tier and review interval.

That's a deliberate choice, not a gap waiting to be filled: a supervisor asking why a specific customer scored "high" gets an answer that names the exact factors and points involved, against a dated ruleset version — the same explainability a reviewable rules engine has always offered, and the property that matters most in an inspection, whether the number came from a rules engine or a trained model.
